## What this code means
The LG dH / FdH / rdH / Er dH code means the refrigerator detected a defrost failure. The evaporator area did not reach the expected defrost temperature within the allowed time, so the unit flagged a frost-removal problem rather than a general cooling fault. LG says this code appears when ice accumulation, a blocked drain hole, a disconnected temperature fuse, a disconnected heater, or a main PCB failure prevents the evaporator from warming up during the defrost cycle.

This is not a compressor or cooling-system fault. It means the automatic defrost sequence could not melt frost off the evaporator coil. If the code is ignored, ice will continue to build, blocking airflow and eventually preventing the refrigerator and freezer from cooling properly.

## Before You Replace Anything

Homeowners often replace the main control board first. Instead, manually defrost the unit fully and inspect the defrost heater and sensor/fuse for continuity before replacing any board.

## Common Causes

- **Ice accumulation and blocked drain hole (~40%)** Heavy frost builds when the drain path freezes or clogs, preventing melt water from leaving the evaporator area during defrost, so the sensor never sees the target temperature.
- **Defrost heater open or disconnected (~25%)** If the heater element is broken or its terminals are disconnected, no heat reaches the evaporator and the defrost cycle times out.
- **Defrost sensor or temperature fuse open (~20%)** An open fuse or out-of-range thermistor tells the board that defrost never completed, even if the heater is working.
- **Wiring harness or connector fault (~10%)** Corroded or loose connectors in the defrost circuit prevent power or signal from reaching the heater or sensor.
- **Main control PCB failure (~5%)** The board may fail to send voltage to the defrost heater during the cycle or misread sensor data, triggering the fault code falsely.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Unplug the refrigerator** and wait 5 minutes, then plug it back in to clear any temporary fault. Let the unit run through one full defrost cycle (typically 8–12 hours) and check whether the code returns.
2. **Inspect for ice buildup** by removing the rear evaporator cover inside the freezer compartment. Look for heavy frost on the coil and a frozen or clogged drain hole beneath the coil.
3. **Manually defrost if needed** by unplugging the unit, opening all doors, and placing towels to catch melt water. Allow all ice to melt completely (6–12 hours), then wipe the drain hole clear and plug the refrigerator back in.
4. **Access and test the defrost heater** (usually mounted under or behind the evaporator coil). With power off, disconnect the heater and measure resistance. An open reading means the heater is broken.
5. **Test the defrost sensor and temperature fuse** assembly, often clipped to the evaporator tubing. Check continuity on both the thermistor and the inline fuse. Replace the assembly if either component is open.
6. **Verify main board output** by entering diagnostic/test mode (consult your model's service manual) and confirming that AC voltage appears at the heater terminals during a forced defrost cycle. If no voltage appears, the main PCB is faulty.
7. **Reassemble and monitor** after replacing the failed component. Run the refrigerator for at least 24 hours and confirm the code does not return and that airflow and cooling are normal.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a professional if you are not comfortable working inside the freezer compartment, handling live AC voltage during diagnostic tests, or interpreting ohm-meter readings on heaters and sensors. A technician has the service manual, the correct resistance tables for your model, and the tools to safely enter diagnostic mode and measure board outputs. If the fault returns after manual defrost or if you see no obvious ice blockage, the repair requires electrical verification of the defrost circuit and possibly a control-board replacement, both of which are best left to a qualified appliance technician.

**Rough cost:** A pro service call runs about $150–350 depending on the part (heater, sensor/fuse, or board) and labor.
