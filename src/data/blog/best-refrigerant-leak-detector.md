## Quick answer

For commercial refrigeration and HVAC service, the **Fieldpiece DR82** is the leak detector to buy — heated diode sensor, sensitivity to 0.1 oz/year, and works on every common refrigerant including R-454B and R-32. If you want a serious detector under $300, the **Inficon TEK-Mate** with its corona-discharge sensor is the right pick. The **Testo 316-3** is the choice for techs who already run a Testo combustion analyzer and want sensor swap support from one vendor. UV dye works as a confirmation tool, not a primary leak detector — use it as a secondary check on confirmed leak sites, not as a replacement.

## What to look for in a refrigerant leak detector

Across 17 years of refrigeration work, I've chased leaks on everything from rooftop split DX systems to multi-evaporator supermarket racks. The leak detector is one of the tools where cheap genuinely doesn't work. Here's what matters:

- **Sensitivity to 0.1 oz/year or better** — EPA Section 608 requires leak detection at this level for the largest commercial systems. Modern detectors achieve 0.05–0.1 oz/year on R-410A and similar. Cheap detectors specify 0.5 oz/year (or worse) and miss small leaks entirely.
- **Sensor type — heated diode vs. corona discharge vs. infrared** — heated diode is the most common modern type, sensitive and selective for halogenated refrigerants. Corona discharge is sensitive but reacts to any gas (false alarms on solvents, alcohol). Infrared is most selective but slowest to respond.
- **Refrigerant compatibility, including A2L (R-454B, R-32) and HC (R-290)** — most modern detectors handle these but verify. R-290 (propane) requires hydrocarbon-compatible electronics for safety.
- **Sensor life and replacement cost** — heated diode sensors typically last 1–2 years of regular use; corona discharge sensors last 2–4 years. Sensor swap is $80–150 depending on detector.
- **Response time** — under 1 second is preferred; 2–3 seconds is workable. Slower than 3 seconds and you lose track of where you swept past the leak.
- **Audible + visual indication** — tone plus LED bar graph; both confirm what you're seeing.
- **Probe length and flexibility** — 18-inch flexible probe is the modern standard; shorter probes don't reach behind walls or under condenser coils.
- **Sensor tip purge / mute function** — the tip will saturate after passing through high-concentration gas. A purge function clears it; without one, you wait for it to clear naturally (30 seconds).

## What to skip

**Skip the $30 Amazon-brand "refrigerant leak detectors."** I've tested two; both used cheap MQ-series MOS sensors that aren't specifically tuned for refrigerants. They react to humidity, body heat, and any solvent in the air. Useless for serious work.

**Skip UV dye as a primary leak detection method.** UV dye works for confirmation: you charge the system with dye, run it for a week, then look for fluorescence at suspected leak sites. It doesn't quickly identify unknown leaks because you have to circulate the dye for days. As a secondary check on a confirmed leak location, it's excellent.

**Skip the older Inficon D-TEK (the 2008-era model).** The original D-TEK had heated diode sensitivity issues; the D-TEK 3 is much better but expensive. The TEK-Mate is the current value pick from Inficon.

## FAQs

**How do I find the actual leak location once the detector alarms?**
Sweep slowly (1 inch per second) with the probe tip 1/4" from the surface. When the detector alarms, back away until it stops, then approach again to localize. The leak is at the point of strongest signal. For tight spots, use a piece of UV dye-coated wire to mark the exact location after confirmation.

**Why is my detector alarming with nothing nearby?**
Background refrigerant from a previous leak or recent service. Either move to clean air to re-baseline, or use the auto-zero / purge function to recalibrate to current background. If it persists, the sensor is contaminated and needs purging or replacement.

**Can a leak detector find an inside-the-coil leak?**
Yes — but you'll need access. Either remove the coil cabinet panels and sweep all accessible coil tube sections, or do a pressure-decay test to identify the affected circuit. For sealed evaporator coils in walls, the only practical approach is to charge with UV dye and let it work over a week of operation.

**My system shows pressure decay but the detector finds no leak. What now?**
Three possibilities: leak rate is below detector sensitivity (under 0.1 oz/year on a long line set), leak location is inaccessible (behind sheet metal you haven't removed), or the "decay" is actually thermal change (system cooling down after operation, pressure drops as temperature drops). Verify with a calibrated pressure transducer over 24 hours of static cooling.

**Heated diode vs. infrared — which is better?**
Heated diode is faster (≤1 second response) and cheaper; infrared is more selective (won't false-alarm on alcohol vapor) and has longer sensor life. For field work, heated diode is the working tech's choice. Infrared is reserved for high-end factory acceptance testing.
