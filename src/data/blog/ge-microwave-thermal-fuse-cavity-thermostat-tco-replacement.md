## GE Microwave Thermal Fuse/Cavity Thermostat (TCO) Replacement — What This Part Does

The thermal fuse or cavity thermostat (TCO) is a temperature-protection device mounted near the magnetron, power transformer, or control panel. It stays closed (allowing current to flow) at normal temperatures, but opens permanently if the microwave overheats. This cuts all power to the unit and prevents a fire. Once a thermal fuse trips, it cannot reset and must be replaced.

These devices fail from actual overheating (blocked vents, failed cooling fan, or magnetron heat), burnt or oxidized spade connectors at the terminals, or internal failure of the cutoff itself. Some microwaves use normally open TCOs that close only when hot, so always verify the correct device type from the wiring diagram before testing. The fuse itself is rarely the problem - the root cause is often poor airflow, a stuck fan, or corroded connections that caused the overheat event.

## Signs It Needs Replacing

- **Microwave completely dead with no lights or display** No interior light, clock, or panel response when plugged in indicates the thermal fuse has opened the circuit.
- **Microwave won't start after a heavy cooking cycle or after running hot** The unit worked fine until a long cook session, then shut off and won't restart.
- **Main fuse or circuit breaker trips repeatedly when microwave is plugged in** A shorted thermal fuse or burnt connector can cause immediate overcurrent when the unit powers on.
- **Burnt smell or visible scorch marks near the magnetron or control area** Overheating or arcing at the thermal fuse terminals leaves visible damage on the housing or wiring.
- **Multimeter shows no continuity across the thermal fuse at room temperature** A normally closed thermal fuse should have zero resistance when cool, so an open reading means it has tripped or failed.
- **Cooling fan runs but microwave still won't power on** Even with airflow working, a previously blown thermal fuse will not self-reset and blocks all operation.

## How to Replace It {#fix}

1. Unplug the microwave from the wall outlet and leave it unplugged during the entire repair.
2. Remove the outer cabinet screws (typically on the back and sides) and slide or lift off the metal cover to expose the internal cavity.
3. Discharge the high-voltage capacitor using an insulated screwdriver across its terminals to safely bleed stored voltage before touching any internal wiring.
4. Locate the thermal fuse or cavity thermostat using the wiring diagram (usually a small cylindrical or disc-shaped device with two spade terminals near the magnetron, transformer, or control board).
5. Disconnect the spade connectors from both terminals of the old thermal fuse and test it with a multimeter in continuity mode (normally closed devices should show zero ohms at room temperature).
6. Inspect the spade connectors and wiring for burnt marks, corrosion, or loose crimps, and replace any damaged terminals with properly crimped and soldered connectors.
7. Check the cooling fan motor for free rotation and verify all vent openings and grilles are clear of dust, debris, or obstructions that could cause future overheating.
8. Install the new thermal fuse or TCO with the same temperature and current ratings as the original, pressing the spade connectors firmly onto both terminals.
9. Reassemble the outer cabinet, restore power, and run a brief test cycle to confirm the microwave starts and heats normally without tripping the new fuse.

## When to Call a Pro

If you are uncomfortable working around high-voltage capacitors or cannot safely discharge the capacitor, call a qualified appliance technician. If the new thermal fuse trips again immediately or the microwave shows signs of arcing, burning, or a failed magnetron, professional diagnosis is required to identify the root cause. Do not bypass or jumper the thermal fuse, as it is a critical fire-safety device.
