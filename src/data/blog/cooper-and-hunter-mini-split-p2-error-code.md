## Cooper & Hunter P2 Error Code — What It Means

The P2 code on your Cooper & Hunter mini-split signals that the compressor over-temperature protection has activated. The unit has detected that the compressor top temperature has climbed too high (typically above 100°C) and has shut down or limited operation to prevent damage. This fault is a protective response, not an immediate failure, but it means the system cannot cool the compressor adequately.

The root cause is almost always a refrigeration or airflow problem. Restricted air movement at either the indoor or outdoor unit, refrigerant leaks, incorrect charge, or a blocked refrigeration circuit all prevent normal heat rejection and force the compressor to run hot. Less commonly, a defective overload protector, wiring fault, or outdoor main PCB can trigger the same code even when physical temperatures are acceptable.

## Common Causes

- **Blocked airflow at indoor or outdoor unit** Dirty filters, clogged coils, debris in air inlets or outlets, or obstructed outdoor condenser fins starve the heat exchangers and trap compressor heat.
- **Refrigerant leak or incorrect charge** Low refrigerant from a leak or improper charge during installation raises discharge pressure and compressor temperature, triggering protection.
- **Blocked refrigeration circuit** A restriction in the tubing or valves prevents refrigerant flow, causing localized overheating and activating the over-temperature cutoff.
- **Outdoor fan or condenser performance issue** A weak or failed outdoor fan motor, seized blade, or fouled condenser coil reduces heat rejection and allows the compressor to overheat.
- **Faulty overload protector or wiring** A defective protector, loose terminal, or broken wire can send a false over-temperature signal to the control board.
- **Defective outdoor main PCB** A failing board may misread compressor-top temperature or fail to modulate compressor speed, causing real or phantom over-temperature faults.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Turn off the unit and wait 10 to 15 minutes** before restoring power to allow the compressor to cool and the fault to clear if it was a transient spike.
2. **Inspect and clean both indoor and outdoor units**. Remove debris from air inlets and outlets, wash or replace the indoor filter, and gently clean the indoor evaporator and outdoor condenser coils with coil cleaner and a soft brush.
3. **Check outdoor fan operation**. With the unit running, confirm the outdoor fan spins freely and moves strong airflow across the condenser. If the fan is weak, slow, or stopped, inspect the motor capacitor and fan motor.
4. **Look for refrigerant leaks**. Examine all flare fittings, service ports, and brazed joints for oil residue. Spray soapy water on suspect areas or use an electronic leak detector. If you find a leak, mark it and call a technician to repair, evacuate, and recharge the system.
5. **Verify compressor temperature and system pressure** (technician task). Using an infrared thermometer, measure the compressor top. If it reads above 100°C and airflow is clean, suspect a refrigerant or circuit problem. Attach manifold gauges to check suction and discharge pressures against the model's specifications.
6. **Test the overload protector** (technician task). Locate the protector on the outdoor unit near the compressor. Disconnect power, unplug the protector's two-wire connector, and measure resistance across the terminals. Normal resistance is approximately 0 Ω. Replace the protector if the reading is infinite or erratic.
7. **Replace the outdoor main PCB if all other checks pass**. If airflow is clear, refrigerant charge and pressures are correct, the protector tests good, and the fault persists, the outdoor control board is the remaining likely cause and should be replaced by a qualified technician.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a licensed HVAC technician if the fault returns after cleaning both units and restoring power, if you see oil at any refrigerant joint (indicating a leak), or if you lack refrigerant-handling tools and certification. Diagnosing compressor over-temperature requires measuring actual compressor-top temperature with an infrared thermometer, checking system pressures with manifold gauges, and sometimes recovering and recharging refrigerant. Replacing the overload protector, servicing the refrigeration circuit, or swapping the outdoor PCB all involve live electrical connections and pressurized refrigerant lines that are unsafe without proper training and equipment. A professional can pinpoint whether the fault is airflow, refrigerant, electrical, or a failing compressor and perform the repair to manufacturer standards.
