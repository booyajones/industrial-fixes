## Bradford White Water Heater Rusty Water — What's Happening

Rusty-colored water from a Bradford White heater is a water quality symptom, not an error code. Bradford White addresses this condition in its FAQ and service bulletins, pointing to corrosion-related issues in the water heater or connected piping rather than electronic or burner failures.

The discoloration is typically rust or iron oxide entering the hot water supply. The most common sources are a depleted anode rod (which removes the tank's sacrificial corrosion protection), internal tank corrosion, or corroded galvanized or steel piping and fittings in the hot water system. Bradford White recommends contacting a plumbing professional if the problem is not resolved.

## Most Likely Causes

- **Spent or depleted anode rod** Once the anode rod is consumed, the tank loses its primary corrosion protection and rust can enter the water supply.
- **Internal tank corrosion** If the tank lining is compromised, rust-colored water comes directly from the tank itself.
- **Corroded galvanized or steel piping and fittings** The discoloration may originate in the plumbing system rather than the heater, especially if rust appears in both hot and cold water.
- **Disturbed sediment after service or water chemistry changes** Rust and mineral deposits can be stirred up during maintenance or when supply water chemistry shifts, releasing discoloration into fixtures.
- **Leaking or corroded tank connections** Rusty fittings or corroded inlet and outlet connections can introduce iron oxide into the hot water path.

## How to Diagnose and Fix {#fix}

1. Confirm whether rusty water appears only in hot water, both hot and cold, or only at certain fixtures to isolate the heater from distribution piping.
2. Inspect the heater and nearby piping visually for visible corrosion, leaks, loose connections, and external rust staining.
3. Drain a sample from the heater drain valve and compare it to water from fixtures to determine whether the discoloration originates in the tank or the plumbing system.
4. Check and inspect the anode rod for significant depletion or corrosion and replace it if the rod is heavily consumed.
5. Inspect the tank for evidence of internal corrosion or active leakage, noting any rust-colored water directly from the drain valve.
6. Flush sediment from the tank if mineral or rust deposits are present, as buildup can stir up and release discoloration.
7. Evaluate connected hot water piping and fittings if the heater itself appears normal but rust persists, since the source may be the building piping rather than the heater.

## When to Call a Pro

Bradford White directs homeowners to contact a plumbing professional if rusty water is unresolved. Anode rod inspection and replacement, tank inspection for internal corrosion, and diagnosis of piping versus tank sources all require specialized tools, safe drain and shutdown procedures, and experience with pressurized water systems. If you see active leakage, severe external corrosion, or if rusty water persists after flushing, call a licensed plumber or water heater technician to evaluate the tank and connected plumbing and determine whether tank replacement is needed. For gas line, burner, or igniter work, or if you ever smell gas, stop and call a licensed technician.
