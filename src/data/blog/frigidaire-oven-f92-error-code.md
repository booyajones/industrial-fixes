## Frigidaire Oven F92 Error Code — What It Means

F92 is a door lock issue code in Frigidaire's F90–F94 family. It tells you the electronic control has detected a problem with the oven door lock or unlock system, not a cooking temperature fault. Frigidaire groups all five codes (F90 through F94) together under door lock problems and recommends a power reset as the first step.

The control expects the lock motor to drive the latch fully and for lock switches to confirm the action. When the door does not lock or unlock as commanded, or the control cannot read correct switch feedback, F92 appears. Common triggers include a worn lock motor, mechanical binding in the latch, debris from self-clean ash, a failed switch, or wiring damage between the lock assembly and the control board.

## Before You Replace Anything

Many homeowners replace the electronic control board first. Test the door lock assembly and inspect wiring connections before ordering a new board, since mechanical and switch faults cause F92 far more often than board failures.

## Common Causes

- **Door lock motor or actuator failure (~45%)** The small motor that drives the latch arm weakens over time or stalls, so the control cannot confirm a full lock or unlock cycle.
- **Latch mechanical binding or misalignment (~25%)** Residue from self-clean cycles, debris in the latch slot, or a door that sits slightly out of square prevents the lock from moving freely.
- **Failed lock switch or open switch circuit (~15%)** Micro switches inside the lock assembly or a door-light switch used in the fault chain develop open contacts or worn actuators.
- **Wiring damage or loose connector (~10%)** Wires between the electronic control and the lock assembly can crack from heat cycling, or connector pins corrode and lose contact.
- **Electronic oven control board fault (~5%)** The main control board misjudges lock feedback or has a failed relay, but this is less common than mechanical and switch problems.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Cancel the cycle and let the oven cool** if it was in self-clean or a high-heat mode, since the lock remains engaged until the cavity drops below safe temperature.
2. **Reset the control** by pressing Cancel/Off, then switch off the circuit breaker for 30–60 seconds to clear transient faults.
3. **Inspect the door and latch area** for racks, foil, debris, or self-clean ash that blocks full closure or binds the latch arm.
4. **Unplug or lock out power**, remove the oven door if needed for access, and take off the front control panel or rear access cover to reach the lock assembly.
5. **Check all connectors** between the electronic control and the door lock assembly for loose pins, corrosion, or cracked insulation, and reseat each plug firmly.
6. **Test the lock motor and switches** by watching the latch arm move when you briefly restore power and command a lock (or measure continuity across motor leads and switch terminals with power off).
7. **Replace the door lock assembly** if the motor does not drive the latch, switches do not change state, or any part of the mechanism is broken or worn.
8. **Install the new assembly**, reconnect wiring, restore door and panels, turn on power, and run a short bake cycle to verify the door locks and unlocks without error.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a technician if you are uncomfortable working with 240 V power or removing the oven door and control panels. A pro should also handle the diagnosis when wiring tests and a new lock assembly do not clear the code, since that points to a control board fault or a less common door-switch issue that requires a wiring diagram and meter work. If your oven is still under warranty or part of a built-in wall installation with tight clearances, professional service avoids accidental damage and preserves coverage.

**Rough cost:** DIY runs about $80–150 in parts, 45–90 min. A pro service call runs about $180–320.
