## Senville EC C1 Error Code — What It Means

EC C1 on a Senville mini split indicates that another indoor unit in your multi-zone system has detected a refrigerant leak. The unit displaying EC C1 is not necessarily the one with the problem. In Senville LETO and AURA multi-zone systems, the zone that actually detects the leak may sound an audible alarm, while the other connected indoor units show EC C1 silently on their displays.

Senville's guidance points to two main scenarios: a temporary false trigger that clears after a full power-off, or a real refrigerant leak or system blockage that requires inspection and repair. The error involves the refrigerant circuit, including copper line sets, flare and braze joints, and internal flow paths.

## Common Causes

- **Temporary sensor glitch** Senville reports that EC C1 can trigger falsely and clear after a 2-minute power-off of all indoor units.
- **Refrigerant leak at line-set connections** Flare fittings, brazed joints, or copper tubing at indoor or outdoor units can develop leaks over time.
- **Leak at indoor unit evaporator or coil** The evaporator coil inside the head unit can corrode or develop pinhole leaks.
- **System blockage in refrigerant circuit** If no leak is visible but the fault persists, Senville's troubleshooting path directs you to check for blockages in the refrigerant lines or components.
- **Leak at outdoor condensing unit** Compressor ports, service valves, or condenser coil joints on the outdoor unit can lose refrigerant.
- **Leak at multi-zone branch box or distributor** In multi-zone installations, the branch box connections are additional potential leak points.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Shut off power** to all indoor units at the breaker or disconnect, wait a full 2 minutes, then restore power and observe whether the fault clears.
2. **Identify the alarming unit** by listening for audible beeping or checking each indoor display, since the zone showing EC C1 may not be the one that detected the leak.
3. **Inspect all refrigerant line connections** at indoor units, outdoor unit service valves, and any branch boxes for visible oil stains, frost, or corrosion that indicates a leak.
4. **Use an electronic leak detector** or soap-bubble solution to confirm the exact location of any suspected leak at flare fittings, braze joints, and coil connections.
5. **Repair the leak** by re-flaring or re-brazing the joint, or replace the damaged line or component if the leak cannot be sealed.
6. **Evacuate the system** with a vacuum pump to at least the level specified in your model's installation manual, typically 500 microns or lower, to remove air and moisture.
7. **Recharge the system** with the exact refrigerant type and weight listed on the outdoor unit nameplate, using manifold gauges to verify proper subcooling and superheat after charging.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a licensed HVAC technician if the error returns after a power cycle, if you lack refrigerant handling certification, or if you cannot locate the leak with visual inspection. Refrigerant recovery, vacuum, and recharge require EPA 608 certification, manifold gauges, a vacuum pump, and a refrigerant scale. Multi-zone systems have multiple potential leak points across branch boxes and line sets, so professional leak detection and pressure testing will save time and prevent undercharging or overcharging the system.
