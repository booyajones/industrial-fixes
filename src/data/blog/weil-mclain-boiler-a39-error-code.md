## Weil-McLain Boiler A39 Error — What It Means

Weil-McLain does not publish a universal meaning for A39 across all boiler families. The code is most likely a lockout or fault-history entry specific to your boiler model and control platform. To find the actual fault, you need to enter the contractor diagnostics menu on your boiler's display, navigate to errors or fault history, and read the stored lockout text that corresponds to A39. The exact fault name and component involved depend on your boiler's CP number and control type.

Because the code mapping varies by model, you should treat A39 as a pointer to a deeper fault record rather than a standalone diagnosis. Weil-McLain directs homeowners to contact a local heating professional for troubleshooting, and technicians are instructed to use the model-specific manual and diagnostic history to map the code to the correct subsystem before replacing parts.

## Before You Replace Anything

Technicians sometimes replace the control board or gas valve without first pulling the fault history and testing the actual failed sensor or switch. Always retrieve the stored lockout text from the diagnostics menu and test the specific component named in the fault before ordering parts.

## Common Causes

- **Flame-proving fault (~30%)** A dirty or misaligned flame sensor, weak gas supply, or ignition problem prevents the burner from sustaining flame and triggers a lockout.
- **Low system pressure or air in the system (~25%)** Pressure below about 12 psi or trapped air stops circulation and can trigger safety logic that logs a fault in the control history.
- **Faulty temperature or low-water sensor (~20%)** A thermistor or low-water cutoff sensor reporting an impossible or out-of-range resistance value causes the boiler to lock out on a sensor fault.
- **Condensate trap blockage or freezing (~15%)** On condensing boilers, a clogged or frozen trap and drain path causes a pressure-switch fault and shutdown until the blockage is cleared.
- **Circulator pump failure or airlock (~10%)** A failed circulator or airlocked system prevents heat transfer and can mimic other faults by causing the boiler to overheat or fail to satisfy the call for heat.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Record your boiler's exact model and CP number** from the nameplate so you can reference the correct manual and control platform.
2. **Enter the contractor diagnostics menu** on the boiler's display (consult your model manual for the key sequence) and navigate to errors or fault history.
3. **Read and write down the stored lockout or fault text** that corresponds to the A39 entry, noting the exact fault name and any component mentioned.
4. **Check basic operating conditions** including thermostat call for heat, gas manual shutoff valve position, system pressure, and visible leaks or obstructions at the vent intake and exhaust.
5. **Test the subsystem identified in the fault** by following the model manual's diagnostic procedure (for example, measure thermistor resistance, inspect and clean the flame sensor, verify circulator operation, or clear the condensate trap).
6. **Repair or replace the root-cause component** identified by the fault history and diagnostic tests, not just the control board.
7. **Clear the fault history and monitor** the boiler through several heating cycles to confirm the repair has resolved the lockout and that no new faults appear.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a professional immediately if you cannot access the diagnostics menu, if the fault history names a gas-valve or ignition component, or if you are uncomfortable working with gas or electrical connections. Technicians have the tools to measure sensor resistance, verify flame current, test gas pressure, and safely diagnose control-board and safety-circuit faults. Because A39 does not have a single published meaning across all Weil-McLain models, a qualified heating contractor is essential to map the code to the correct fault, test the named component, and perform the repair without guessing or replacing parts unnecessarily.

**Rough cost:** A pro service call runs about $150-400.
