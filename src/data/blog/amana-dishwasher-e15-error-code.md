## What this code means
The E15 code on Amana HVAC equipment indicates a low-pressure fault in the refrigeration system. This fault is stored by the control after the system sees repeated low-pressure operation. According to Amana service documentation, the control stores the E15 fault after three consecutive times the low-pressure condition occurs. This is not a single transient event but a pattern that points to an underlying refrigeration or airflow issue.

The code signals that system pressure in the evaporator is running below normal operating range. Common causes include refrigerant loss, restricted airflow over the indoor coil, dirty filters, or mechanical problems in the refrigeration circuit. The fault is designed to protect the compressor and prevent damage from prolonged low-pressure operation. Note that E15 on Amana dishwashers means something entirely different (a leak code), so verify you are working with an HVAC unit before following this guide.

## Before You Replace Anything

Homeowners often assume the control board is bad and replace it first. Before ordering any board, check airflow (filter, indoor coil cleanliness) and have a technician verify refrigerant pressures with gauges. Most E15 faults trace to refrigerant or airflow, not electronics.

## Common Causes

- **Low refrigerant charge (~50%)** A leak or undercharge reduces evaporator pressure, triggering the fault after three consecutive occurrences.
- **Restricted indoor airflow (~30%)** Dirty filters, blocked return grilles, or a dirty evaporator coil starve the coil of air and drop refrigerant pressure.
- **Dirty or iced evaporator coil (~10%)** Ice buildup or dust on the coil reduces heat exchange and drives evaporator pressure down.
- **Faulty low-pressure switch or sensor (~5%)** A failing pressure transducer or switch may report low pressure even when the system is running normally.
- **Metering device restriction (~3%)** A clogged expansion valve or fixed orifice can starve the evaporator and cause low suction pressure.
- **Control board logic issue (~2%)** Rarely, the control misreads pressure inputs or stores the fault incorrectly due to a board defect.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Verify the fault history** by pressing the FAULT RECALL button on the control board or thermostat interface (consult your unit's service label for the exact button sequence). Note how many times E15 has been stored and when it first appeared.
2. **Turn off power** at the breaker and inspect the indoor air filter. Replace it if dirty or overdue. Check all return grilles and registers for blockages (furniture, closed dampers).
3. **Inspect the indoor evaporator coil** for ice, dust, or other obstructions. If ice is present, turn off the system and allow it to thaw completely before restarting. Clean the coil with approved coil cleaner if accessible.
4. **Call a licensed HVAC technician** to attach manifold gauges and measure live suction and discharge pressures. The technician will compare readings to the nameplate pressures and refrigerant type (R-410A, R-22, or other) for your model.
5. **Repair any refrigerant leaks** found during the pressure test. The technician will use electronic leak detection or nitrogen pressure testing, then evacuate and recharge the system to the correct weight listed on the data plate.
6. **Check the metering device** (expansion valve or fixed orifice) for debris or ice. If the valve is stuck or the orifice is clogged, clean or replace it according to the service manual for your model.
7. **Clear the fault history** after repairs are complete (using the FAULT RECALL function) and run the system through a full cooling cycle. Monitor for at least 24 hours to confirm the E15 does not return. The control only stores the fault after three consecutive occurrences, so a single test cycle may not prove the fix.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a professional immediately if you see the E15 code. Diagnosing low-pressure faults requires manifold gauges, refrigerant handling certification, and knowledge of superheat and subcooling targets for your specific model. Attempting to add refrigerant without finding and fixing a leak will waste money and may damage the compressor. A technician will measure live pressures, perform leak detection, verify airflow with temperature splits, and restore the correct refrigerant charge. If the fault recurs after filter replacement and coil cleaning, only a licensed HVAC contractor has the tools and training to pinpoint metering-device restrictions, failing sensors, or control-board logic problems. Refrigerant work is also regulated by EPA Section 608, so DIY refrigerant service is both unsafe and illegal.

**Rough cost:** A pro service call runs about $200-$600.
