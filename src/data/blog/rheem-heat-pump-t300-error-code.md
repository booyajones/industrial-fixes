## Rheem Heat Pump T300 Error — What It Means

The T300 error code on a Rheem heat pump indicates a problem with one of the temperature sensors in the system. The exact sensor varies by model, but often involves the outdoor coil sensor, outdoor ambient sensor, or a refrigerant line sensor. The control board is either receiving no signal from the sensor, getting a reading outside the normal range, or detecting a short or open circuit in the sensor wiring.

Because sensor designations and error code meanings can differ across Rheem heat pump models and firmware versions, consult your owner's manual or the wiring diagram inside the unit to confirm which sensor T300 refers to on your specific model. The fix typically involves inspecting the sensor, its connector, and the wiring harness for damage or corrosion.

## Before You Replace Anything

Homeowners sometimes replace the control board when the real problem is a loose sensor plug or corroded connector. Test sensor resistance with a multimeter and inspect all connectors before ordering a board.

## Common Causes

- **Loose or corroded sensor connector (~35%)** Vibration, weather exposure, or improper installation can cause the plug at the sensor or control board to work loose or develop corrosion that breaks the circuit.
- **Failed temperature sensor (~30%)** The thermistor inside the sensor can drift out of specification or fail open or shorted, sending incorrect resistance readings to the board.
- **Damaged sensor wiring (~20%)** Rodent damage, sharp metal edges, or freeze-thaw cycles can cut or fray the wires between the sensor and the control board.
- **Water intrusion at sensor (~10%)** Moisture entering the sensor housing or connector can create a short or intermittent fault that triggers the code.
- **Defective control board (~5%)** The board's sensor input circuit can fail and misread a good sensor, though this is less common than wiring or sensor issues.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Turn off power** to the heat pump at the breaker and the outdoor disconnect switch.
2. **Locate the sensor** identified by your model's manual or wiring diagram (typically mounted on the outdoor coil or refrigerant line).
3. **Unplug the sensor connector** at both the sensor end and the control board end, then inspect pins and sockets for corrosion or bent contacts.
4. **Clean connectors** with electrical contact cleaner and reseat them firmly, then restore power and check if the code clears.
5. **Test sensor resistance** with a multimeter if the code persists; compare the reading to the manufacturer's temperature-resistance chart for your model.
6. **Replace the sensor** if the resistance is out of range or the sensor shows physical damage or water intrusion.
7. **Clear the error code** using the control panel or by cycling power, then run the heat pump through a full cycle to confirm normal operation.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a professional if you are uncomfortable working with electrical connections, if the sensor tests good but the code persists, or if you need to access the refrigerant circuit to replace a suction-line sensor. A technician can also use diagnostic software to read live sensor values and pinpoint intermittent faults. If the control board itself is suspect after ruling out sensor and wiring problems, a pro can confirm the diagnosis and handle the board replacement, which may require reconfiguration or firmware updates.

**Rough cost:** DIY runs about $30-80 in parts, 20-45 min. A pro service call runs about $150-350.
