## Fujitsu E:59 Error Code — What It Means

E:59 is not universally documented across all Fujitsu mini-split model families in public manufacturer materials. It falls into the same general error category as indoor sensor and controller board faults. The exact meaning depends on your specific model series and should be verified against your service manual or remote-control diagnostic table. Based on similar Fujitsu sensor codes, E:59 most likely indicates a problem with the indoor coil thermistor circuit, such as a shorted sensor, open wiring, or a controller PCB that cannot read the sensor correctly.

Because Fujitsu uses different fault-code tables for different model families, always cross-reference your exact model number with the manufacturer's documentation before replacing parts. Sensor faults in this family typically involve the indoor heat-exchanger thermistor, its harness and connectors, or the indoor controller board itself.

## Common Causes

- **Shorted or open indoor coil thermistor** The sensor attached to the indoor heat exchanger reads abnormally low or infinite resistance, triggering the fault.
- **Loose, damaged, or corroded sensor connector** The harness connection between the thermistor and the controller PCB has poor contact, moisture intrusion, or broken pins.
- **Defective indoor controller PCB** The main circuit board cannot interpret the sensor signal correctly even when the sensor and wiring test within spec.
- **Broken or open sensor harness wiring** The conductors running from the indoor coil sensor to the PCB have been damaged, pinched, or severed.
- **Moisture or water intrusion at sensor location** Condensate overflow or drain backup causes corrosion or short circuits in the thermistor circuit.
- **Restricted airflow or icing masking a sensor fault** Dirty filters or a fouled coil can cause abnormal coil temperatures that trigger or accompany thermistor errors.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Confirm the exact fault code** by consulting your specific Fujitsu model's service manual or diagnostic table, because error definitions vary by series.
2. **Power off the system completely** at the circuit breaker for two minutes, then restore power and check if the code clears or returns immediately.
3. **Inspect indoor airflow** by checking the air filter for dirt, the coil for blockage or ice, and the discharge and return vents for obstructions.
4. **Check the indoor coil thermistor connector** for looseness, pin damage, moisture, or corrosion where the sensor plugs into the controller board.
5. **Measure the thermistor resistance** using an ohmmeter across the sensor terminals and compare the reading to the manufacturer's resistance chart for your model.
6. **Test the sensor harness continuity** from the thermistor all the way to the controller PCB to rule out open or broken wiring.
7. **Replace the faulty component** (thermistor if out of spec, harness if damaged, or indoor PCB if sensor and wiring test good but the fault persists), then clear the code and run a full heating and cooling cycle to verify the repair.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a licensed HVAC technician if you are not comfortable working with low-voltage electronics, do not own a digital multimeter, or cannot locate your model's service manual and thermistor resistance chart. Also call a professional if the fault persists after you have verified the sensor and wiring are good, because indoor controller PCB diagnosis and refrigerant-circuit interaction require specialized training. If you see signs of water damage, icing, or refrigerant leaks alongside the error code, a technician should evaluate the entire system to prevent compressor or expansion-valve damage.
