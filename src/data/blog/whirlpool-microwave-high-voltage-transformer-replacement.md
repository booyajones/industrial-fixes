## Whirlpool Microwave High-Voltage Transformer Replacement Guide — What This Part Does

The high-voltage transformer steps up standard 120 VAC household voltage to the several thousand volts the magnetron needs to generate microwaves and heat food. It sits in the high-voltage circuit alongside the capacitor and magnetron. When the transformer fails, the magnetron receives no drive power and the oven runs but does not heat.

Transformers fail from normal aging, overheating from a shorted capacitor or magnetron, loose or burnt wiring connections on the primary side, and voltage surges. A failed transformer may show open or high-resistance windings, visible charring or discoloration on the coil assembly, or burnt terminal connections. A shorted capacitor or failing magnetron can also damage a good transformer over time by drawing excessive current through the secondary windings.

## Signs It Needs Replacing

- **Microwave runs but food does not heat** The turntable spins and lights work but food stays cold because the transformer is not supplying high voltage to the magnetron.
- **Loud humming or buzzing with no heat** The transformer hums loudly during operation but does not produce secondary voltage due to open or damaged windings.
- **Blown line fuse after replacing it once** A shorted transformer winding or failed capacitor in the high-voltage circuit repeatedly blows the main fuse.
- **Burning smell or visible char marks on the transformer** Overheating from a failed capacitor or magnetron leaves burn marks, discoloration, or a burnt odor on the transformer body.
- **Transformer primary measures above 1 ohm resistance** When tested unpowered, the primary winding shows resistance higher than 1 ohm or reads open, indicating winding failure.
- **High-voltage winding reads outside 35 to 150 ohms** The secondary winding resistance falls outside the normal 35 to 150 ohm range, showing the coil is open or shorted.

## How to Replace It {#fix}

1. Unplug the microwave from the wall outlet and leave it unplugged for at least five minutes to allow internal capacitors to begin discharging.
2. Remove the outer cabinet by taking out the screws along the top, sides, and back panel, then lift or slide the housing off the chassis.
3. Discharge the high-voltage capacitor by shorting both terminals to the metal chassis using an insulated screwdriver with one hand, keeping your other hand and body clear of all high-voltage components.
4. Label and photograph all wire connections on the transformer primary and secondary terminals, then disconnect the spade or quick-connect terminals by pulling straight off.
5. Remove the mounting bolts or screws that secure the transformer to the chassis or mounting bracket and lift the old transformer out of the cavity.
6. Position the new high-voltage transformer in the same orientation as the old unit and fasten it to the chassis with the mounting hardware.
7. Reconnect all primary and secondary wiring to the correct terminals using your labels and photos, pressing spade connectors fully onto the terminals until they click or seat firmly.
8. Reinstall the outer cabinet by aligning the housing over the chassis and replacing all screws in the reverse order of removal.
9. Plug the microwave back in, run a one-minute heating test with a cup of water inside, and verify the water heats and the unit operates normally without humming or odor.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a technician if you are uncomfortable working around high-voltage components or if you do not own a multimeter to verify that the capacitor is discharged and to measure primary input voltage at the transformer. If you measure close to 120 VAC at the transformer primary during a test run but the oven still does not heat, the fault may lie in the capacitor, magnetron, or control board, and a technician with high-voltage test equipment can isolate the failure quickly. Any visible arcing, repeated fuse failure after transformer replacement, or uncertainty about safely discharging the capacitor warrants professional service.
