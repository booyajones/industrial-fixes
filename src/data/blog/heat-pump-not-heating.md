Heat pumps are the most misunderstood HVAC equipment in residential homes. When they stop heating, homeowners often assume the worst — but half the time, the unit is working exactly as designed. This guide walks you through every cause of a heat pump not heating, starting with the ones you can fix yourself in 10 minutes.

## What Does "Heat Pump Not Heating" Mean?

A heat pump moves heat rather than generating it. In heating mode, it extracts heat from outdoor air (even cold air contains heat energy) and transfers it inside. When it stops heating, the cause is usually one of these:

1. **Defrost mode** — the unit is temporarily pausing heating to melt ice off the outdoor coil (normal operation)
2. **Outdoor temperature lockout** — it's too cold outside for the heat pump to operate efficiently
3. **Reversing valve failure** — the valve that switches between heating and cooling is stuck
4. **Low refrigerant** — a leak has reduced the refrigerant charge
5. **Failed capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor won't start
6. **Auxiliary heat not staging** — backup electric heat strips aren't activating when needed

Understanding which one you're dealing with determines whether you need a screwdriver or a service call.

### The Defrost Mode Misunderstanding

This alone accounts for a huge percentage of "heat pump not heating" service calls. In cold weather, moisture from outdoor air freezes onto the outdoor coil. When ice buildup gets thick enough, it blocks airflow and kills efficiency. The heat pump's defrost control board detects this and temporarily:

- Switches the reversing valve to cooling mode (the outdoor coil becomes the condenser)
- Turns off the outdoor fan
- Activates the emergency/auxiliary heat strips to maintain indoor temperature

From inside the house, defrost mode looks and sounds alarming. The outdoor unit steams (melting ice), the system feels less effective, and sometimes you'll see a light labeled "DEFROST" or "AUX HEAT" on your thermostat. **This is normal.** A defrost cycle typically lasts 5–15 minutes.

If your heat pump appears to be in defrost mode more than once per hour, or if a single defrost cycle lasts more than 20 minutes, that's abnormal and suggests a problem with the defrost board, defrost thermostat, or outdoor fan motor.

## How to Fix It

### Step 1: Check If It's Defrost Mode (5 minutes)

Go outside and look at the outdoor unit.

- **Light frost or ice on the coil:** Normal in cold weather. Check again in 15 minutes — it should be gone.
- **Heavy ice covering the entire unit, including the top:** Abnormal. The unit is stuck in a freeze condition. This can happen from a failed defrost board, a blocked outdoor coil (debris, landscaping too close), or very low airflow due to dirty filter.
- **No ice, fan running:** Not a defrost issue. Continue to Step 2.
- **Outdoor unit not running at all:** Go to Step 4 (capacitor check).

### Step 2: Check the Outdoor Temperature

Heat pumps become significantly less efficient below 35°F and most standard heat pumps have a lockout (either automatic or manual) below 0–10°F depending on the manufacturer. Below this point, the system relies entirely on auxiliary electric heat strips.

**What to do:** Check your thermostat for an "AUX HEAT" or "EMERGENCY HEAT" indicator.

- **AUX HEAT on:** The heat pump is running but getting help from the electric strips. This is normal when it's very cold outside.
- **EMERGENCY HEAT on:** The heat pump is bypassed entirely and only electric heat strips are running. This is very expensive to operate continuously — it's meant for emergency use when the heat pump itself fails.

If your home isn't reaching setpoint even with AUX HEAT running, your heat strips may be undersized or one of the strips may have failed. An HVAC tech can check strip amperage with a clamp meter.

### Step 3: Listen for the Reversing Valve

The reversing valve is a solenoid-operated valve that routes refrigerant flow in two directions — one for heating, one for cooling. When it sticks or fails, the system gets "confused" and either stays in cooling mode (blowing cold air when you want heat) or can't switch at all.

**Diagnosis:** Set your thermostat to COOLING mode for 2 minutes, then switch back to HEATING mode. Listen at the air handler for a click or thump sound when you switch modes — that's the reversing valve actuating. If you hear nothing, the valve solenoid or valve body may be failed.

**Another clue:** If your heat pump cools normally in summer but won't heat in winter (or vice versa), a stuck reversing valve is the top suspect.

Reversing valve replacement is a refrigerant system job — it requires an EPA 608-certified technician. This is not a DIY repair.

### Step 4: Check the Capacitor

The capacitor is a cylindrical component inside the outdoor unit's electrical compartment. It provides the starting torque for the compressor and outdoor fan motor. When it fails:

- The compressor may hum but not start
- The outdoor fan may not spin (you'll see the compressor is running but the fan is stationary)
- The unit may try to start, fail, and trip the breaker

**How to check (safely):** Turn off power at the outdoor disconnect before opening the electrical compartment. Never touch capacitor terminals — even after power is off, a capacitor holds a charge. Use an insulated screwdriver to short across the terminals before touching anything.

A technician can test capacitance with a multimeter set to the capacitance function. A capacitor rated at 45/5 µF that reads 38/4.2 µF is marginal and should be replaced. Most capacitors last 5–10 years.

### Step 5: Check Refrigerant Charge

Low refrigerant (due to a leak) is one of the most common causes of a heat pump that runs but doesn't heat effectively. Signs of low refrigerant:

- Ice forming on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil
- The system runs constantly but never reaches setpoint
- Significantly higher electric bills with lower comfort
- Hissing or bubbling sound near the refrigerant lines

**You cannot check or add refrigerant yourself** — refrigerant handling requires EPA 608 certification and specialized gauges. But you can inspect the refrigerant lines for oily residue (a sign of a leak) at accessible connection points.

### Step 6: Check the Air Filter and Indoor Coil

A clogged filter reduces the airflow over the indoor coil. In heating mode, this means less heat gets transferred into the house. Replace the filter if it's dirty. While you're at it, look at the indoor coil (the evaporator coil in the air handler). If it's coated in dust and grime, airflow is severely restricted. Coil cleaning is a technician job.

### Step 7: Check the Auxiliary Heat Strips

If you suspect the electric heat strips aren't working, set the thermostat to EMERGENCY HEAT mode. This bypasses the heat pump and runs only the strips. If the house warms up on emergency heat but not normal heat pump mode, the heat pump itself is the problem. If emergency heat also doesn't warm the house, one or more heat strips have failed.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a licensed HVAC technician when:

- **The outdoor unit is completely encased in ice** and doesn't clear after 30 minutes with the unit off. This often indicates a refrigerant issue or a failed defrost system that a tech needs to diagnose.
- **You suspect low refrigerant.** Adding refrigerant without finding and fixing the leak first is a waste of money and refrigerant. A certified tech will find the leak, repair it, evacuate the system, and recharge to the correct specification.
- **The reversing valve needs replacement.** This requires cutting and brazing refrigerant lines — not a DIY job.
- **The compressor won't start even after capacitor replacement.** A failed compressor means a major repair or replacement decision.
- **The system is more than 10 years old** and requires a refrigerant repair. Older units use R-22 (Freon), which is extremely expensive now that production has ended. A single R-22 recharge can cost $500–$800. At that point, replacement often makes more financial sense.

**Average repair costs:**
- Capacitor replacement: $150–$250
- Defrost board: $200–$400
- Reversing valve: $500–$1,000
- Refrigerant leak repair + recharge: $400–$1,500 depending on refrigerant type
- Compressor replacement: $1,200–$2,500

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: My heat pump blows cool air in heating mode. Is that normal?**

A: Heat pump supply air (60–90°F) feels cooler than gas furnace supply air (120–140°F). This is normal — it still heats your home, just more slowly and at a lower temperature differential. However, if air at the vent feels genuinely cold (below 75°F when the setpoint is 70°F), something is wrong — likely a refrigerant issue or the reversing valve is stuck in cooling mode.

**Q: My heat pump runs constantly and never reaches setpoint. What's wrong?**

A: Several possible causes. If it's below 35°F outside, the heat pump may simply be undersized for your climate — this is a design issue, not a failure. If it's above 35°F and the unit won't keep up, suspect: low refrigerant charge, dirty coil reducing efficiency, a failing compressor, or a home with poor insulation. Start by replacing the filter and checking outdoor temperature, then call a tech for a refrigerant check.

**Q: My thermostat shows "AUX HEAT" all the time. Is something wrong?**

A: Auxiliary heat is normal when outdoor temperatures drop below 35–40°F. It's a sign your heat pump is working correctly — it's calling for backup heat because it can't keep up alone. If AUX HEAT is running constantly at mild temperatures (above 45°F), something is wrong with the heat pump. Common causes: low refrigerant, failed reversing valve, or a failing compressor.

**Q: The outdoor fan isn't spinning but I can hear the compressor. What does that mean?**

A: Almost certainly a failed capacitor. The fan and compressor share a dual-run capacitor. When the capacitor degrades, it often loses the fan side first because fans have lower starting torque requirements. A failed fan with a running compressor will cause the unit to overheat quickly. Turn it off immediately and replace the capacitor. This is one of the easier DIY repairs on a heat pump.

**Q: How do I know if my heat pump is in heating or cooling mode?**

A: Set your thermostat to HEAT. In heating mode, the indoor coil (in the air handler or furnace) is the condenser — it gets warm. You can feel warm air at the vents. The outdoor coil gets cold and may frost up. In cooling mode, the indoor coil is cold and dehumidifies, while the outdoor coil gets warm. If your indoor air is cold in HEAT mode, the reversing valve is stuck in cooling position.
