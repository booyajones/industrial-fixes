## Samsung Oven C-d1 Error Code — What It Means

The C-d1 code indicates a door lock mechanism problem. The oven control board is not receiving the expected signal from the door latch or lock assembly, so it believes the door is not properly closed or locked. This fault prevents the oven from starting a cycle or heating because Samsung ovens require confirmation that the door is secure, especially after self-clean or high-heat operations.

The code points to a mechanical or electrical failure in the door lock system rather than a heating element or temperature issue. The lock assembly includes a small motor (actuator), a cam or latch rod, and a position switch. If any of these components fail, stick, or lose electrical connection, the control board cannot confirm door status and throws C-d1.

## Before You Replace Anything

Many people replace the main control board first, assuming an electronics fault. Test the door lock motor for continuity and check that the latch moves freely before spending money on a board.

## Common Causes

- **Faulty door lock assembly or actuator motor (~50%)** The lock motor wears out, sticks, or burns out from repeated high-heat cycles, preventing the latch from moving to the locked position.
- **Stuck latch or cam mechanism (~25%)** The lock rod or cam jams after a self-clean cycle or due to grease and debris buildup, so the motor cannot drive it fully into place.
- **Loose, damaged, or disconnected wiring (~15%)** Harness connectors at the lock assembly or control board vibrate loose, corrode, or suffer heat damage, breaking the signal path.
- **Debris or door misalignment (~8%)** Food spills, grease, or a bent hinge prevent the door from closing fully or block the latch striker, so the switch never closes.
- **Control board lock-drive circuit failure (~2%)** The relay or transistor that powers the lock motor on the main board fails, even though the lock assembly itself is good.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Disconnect power** at the circuit breaker or unplug the range and wait at least five minutes to clear any temporary control fault.
2. **Inspect the door** for proper closure, check hinges for damage or misalignment, and clean any grease or debris from the latch striker and door seal area.
3. **Remove the control panel or rear access cover** (consult your model's service manual for screw locations) to expose the door lock assembly and wiring harness.
4. **Examine the lock mechanism** for physical obstructions, verify the latch rod or cam moves freely by hand, and look for signs of melting, corrosion, or burned connectors.
5. **Test the lock motor and switch** using a multimeter set to continuity mode: disconnect the harness, check the motor windings for an open circuit, and verify the position switch changes state when the latch is moved manually.
6. **Replace the door lock assembly** if the motor fails continuity, the switch does not toggle, or the mechanism is physically jammed and cannot be freed.
7. **Reconnect all wiring**, restore power, and run a test cycle to confirm the lock engages smoothly and the C-d1 code does not return; if the code persists with a new lock, suspect the control board lock-drive circuit.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a technician if you are uncomfortable working inside the control panel with line voltage present, if the lock assembly replacement does not clear the code and you suspect a control board fault, or if your oven uses a complex dual-lock system that requires calibration after installation. Also call for help if the wiring diagram for your specific model is unclear and you cannot identify which harness connects to the lock circuit. Professional diagnosis with a service manual and proper test equipment can save the cost of trial-and-error part swaps when multiple components test marginal.

**Rough cost:** DIY runs about $50–120 in parts, 45–90 min. A pro service call runs about $150–280.
