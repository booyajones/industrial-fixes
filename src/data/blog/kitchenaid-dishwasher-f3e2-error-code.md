## KitchenAid F3E2 Error Code — What It Means

F3E2 on a KitchenAid oven or range signals an oven temperature sensor problem. The control board is not receiving the expected resistance or feedback from the oven temperature sensor circuit, or it detects a condition it interprets as a sensor fault. This can be an open sensor, an out-of-range reading, a wiring issue, or less commonly a control board malfunction.

KitchenAid's official product-help pages describe F3 and F3E2 codes as oven-control or wiring-related faults on ranges. The code may appear during preheating, self-clean, or normal operation, and it usually disables the oven until the fault is cleared. A power reset is the first troubleshooting step recommended by the manufacturer.

## Before You Replace Anything

Many owners replace the electronic control board first. Always test the sensor resistance with a multimeter and inspect the harness connectors before ordering a new control.

## Common Causes

- **Faulty oven temperature sensor (~50%)** The sensor probe fails open, drifts out of range, or develops internal corrosion that breaks the circuit.
- **Loose, damaged, or burned wiring and connectors (~30%)** Heat cycling, vibration, or physical damage causes intermittent or open connections between the sensor and control board.
- **Control board misreading or malfunction (~15%)** The oven control itself fails to interpret a good sensor signal correctly, often due to relay or processor faults.
- **True over-temperature condition (~5%)** An actual runaway oven temperature can trigger F3E2 as a protective fault in some KitchenAid models.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Kill power at the circuit breaker.** Wait at least one minute to discharge capacitors and reset the control. KitchenAid recommends this as the first step for F3 and F3E2 faults.
2. **Restore power and observe.** If the code does not return immediately, monitor the oven through one bake or preheat cycle. If F3E2 reappears, continue diagnostics.
3. **Access the oven temperature sensor.** Remove the oven racks and locate the sensor probe, usually a metal rod protruding into the oven cavity at the upper rear wall. Remove the rear interior panel or back panel to reach the sensor mounting bracket and harness connector.
4. **Inspect the sensor, wiring, and connector.** Look for heat damage, melted insulation, loose pins, or corrosion at the plug. Wiggle the connector while watching for intermittent contact. Check that no wires are pinched behind the oven liner.
5. **Measure sensor resistance with a multimeter.** Unplug the sensor from the harness and set your meter to ohms. At room temperature, field experience suggests readings around 1,000 to 1,100 ohms for many models, but consult your model's wiring diagram for the exact specification. An open (infinite) or wildly fluctuating reading means replace the sensor.
6. **Test harness continuity.** If the sensor tests good, check continuity from the harness plug all the way back to the control board. Repair or replace any damaged wiring or connectors.
7. **Replace the failed component.** Install the new sensor or control board, reconnect all harness plugs, reassemble panels, and restore power. Clear any stored fault codes by power-cycling, then run a test bake to verify normal operation.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a qualified appliance technician if you are uncomfortable working inside a 240 V appliance, if the rear oven panels are difficult to remove, or if the sensor and wiring both test good but the code remains. Technicians carry model-specific wiring diagrams, can perform live voltage checks at the control board, and have access to OEM control boards that match your exact software version. A pro visit is also wise if the oven has recently experienced a true over-temperature event, since that may indicate a stuck relay or failed safety circuit that requires board-level diagnosis.

**Rough cost:** DIY runs about $25–70 for sensor, 20–40 min. A pro service call runs about $150–280 service call plus parts.
