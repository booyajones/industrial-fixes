## What this code means
The F6E4 code on Whirlpool dishwashers is a float or overfill safety fault. The control is detecting that the overfill float switch is open or activated, meaning the machine believes water has reached a protected overfill condition. Whirlpool product help states that incorrect leveling of the dishwasher can cause this error because it can actuate the overfill or floater switch. In technician terms, the control sees the float switch circuit open or a condition that makes it think the machine is overfilling.

This code protects against flooding by shutting down the wash cycle when the float mechanism in the base pan lifts. The float can be triggered by actual water in the base from a leak, by an unlevel installation that tilts the float assembly, or by a stuck or failing float switch. Less commonly, wiring issues between the float and control or a failed control itself can trigger the fault even when no overfill condition exists.

## Before You Replace Anything

Homeowners often replace the main control board first. Check that the dishwasher is level front-to-back and side-to-side, and pull the unit forward to inspect the base pan for standing water before buying any parts.

## Common Causes

- **Dishwasher not level (~35%)** Incorrect leveling, especially after installation or flooring or cabinet changes, can tilt the float mechanism and actuate the overfill switch even with no water present.
- **Water in the base pan or spill tray (~30%)** A leak into the base from the door gasket, inlet hose, drain hose, or tub seal lifts the float and triggers the safety circuit.
- **Stuck, obstructed, or failing overfill float switch (~20%)** The float mechanism can stick in the up position due to debris, corrosion, or mechanical failure, causing the control to see a constant overfill signal.
- **Wiring harness or connector issue (~10%)** Damaged wiring, loose terminals, or high resistance between the float switch and control can cause an open circuit that the control interprets as an overfill condition.
- **Failed main control board (~5%)** If the float circuit and wiring test good but the fault persists, the control itself may be misreading the float signal.

## Step-by-Step Fix {#fix}

1. **Kill power** at the circuit breaker or unplug the dishwasher before any inspection or repair work.
2. **Check level** by placing a bubble level on the door opening and across the top of the unit, and adjust the front leveling legs and rear supports until the dishwasher is level front-to-back and side-to-side.
3. **Pull the unit forward** and remove the lower access panel or toe kick to inspect the base pan or spill tray for standing water.
4. **Dry the base pan** completely if water is present, then inspect the door gasket, inlet hose, drain hose, and all hose connections for signs of leakage.
5. **Inspect the float mechanism** in the base pan for free movement, debris, or corrosion, and confirm it moves up and down without sticking.
6. **Check the wiring harness and connectors** between the float switch and the main control for damage, loose terminals, or signs of moisture or corrosion.
7. **Replace the overfill float switch assembly** if the float is stuck, damaged, or the circuit tests open when the float is down, then restore power and run a short test cycle to confirm the fault clears.

## When to Call a Pro

Call a professional if you are uncomfortable working with the dishwasher's wiring or pulling the unit out from under the counter. Also call a pro if you have leveled the machine, dried the base pan, confirmed the float moves freely, inspected all hoses and gaskets without finding a leak, and checked the wiring harness without finding damage, yet the F6E4 code persists. At that point the issue is likely a failed float switch or main control that requires component-level testing and part replacement. A qualified appliance technician has the tools and experience to test the float circuit against the model's wiring diagram and to replace the control board if necessary.

**Rough cost:** DIY runs about $15–75 in parts, 30–90 min. A pro service call runs about $150–300.
